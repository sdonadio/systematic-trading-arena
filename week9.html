Integration & Demo — Season Finale

Nothing new to build; everything to connect. Tonight three independent processes run as one market, the bugs that live in the seams come out, and the leaderboard locks on risk-adjusted return.

  • Trace the whole system — every arrow between Yahoo, broker, exchange, trader, dashboard and score.
  • Survive partial failure — stale feeds, dropped sockets, rejected orders, duplicate fills.
  • Defend your book with a tearsheet, and know why the steadiest curve wins the season.
What is it?

Integration correctness is a different claim from unit correctness

Every part passing its own tests does not mean the system works. Bugs live in the seams between components that are each, individually, correct.

Unit correctness means each object honours its own contract in isolation — roughly 1,280 tests do that for the platform. Integration correctness means the objects honour each other's contracts when wired together, live, under load. The second does not follow from the first.

The classic seam bugs all have the same shape: two correct components disagree about timing or units. The signal fires faster than risk can veto it. A fill arrives before its ack. Two messages race on the same book. One side sends a price as a float, the other rounds to ticks — and both are right by their own spec.

In AlgoArena the seams are explicit, which is the whole reason the wire protocol is Pydantic. Three processes speak only models from shared/messages.py; if a message round-trips, the seam holds. So test the conversation, not just the speakers.

How does it work?

Live systems fail partially. Design for degradation.

A live system rarely dies all at once. The socket stays up while the data goes stale; the order is rejected while the process is healthy. Detect, do not assume.

Why does it matter in trading?

Raw return is the most misleading number in finance

The same +12% can be skill or a coin flip that landed right. exchange/scoring.py ranks return per unit of volatility, with a drawdown penalty — announced in Week 1, and it holds tonight.

A tearsheet is one page that answers two questions: did this make money, and how much risk did it take to do it? The equity curve tells the story before any number does — a smooth 20% beats a jagged 40%, because the second is one bad tick from a blow-up. Then the numbers: Sharpe (return per unit of risk), max drawdown (the worst pain an investor had to sit through), Calmar, turnover, exposure.

# exchange/scoring.py  (score_equity)
r      = returns(equity)        # per-snapshot returns
vol    = pstdev(r)              # volatility of the book
mdd    = max_drawdown(equity)   # worst peak-to-trough
excess = mean(r) - RISK_FREE    # above idle cash
ratio  = excess / max(vol, VOL_FLOOR)
score  = ratio * max(0, 1 - PENALTY*mdd)   # PENALTY = 2.0
# ranked PER ROLE: traders vs traders, brokers vs brokers
Excess return over idle cash is why parking your cash and doing nothing scores about zero rather than winning. The drawdown penalty always hurts: a 25% max drawdown halves your score.

A great system badly explained loses to an average one told well.

Lead with the thesis — "I make markets and earn the spread" — then show the tearsheet that proves it. Numbers support a narrative; they are not one. Be ready for "why this and not that?": every choice, from spread width to stop level, was a trade-off. Name the trade-off. And keep a recorded rehearsal as your safety net for when the demo gods are unkind.

Common mistakes

Five ways to lose the finale with working code

None of these is a logic bug. Each expands.

Debugging live for the first time
The finale is not a rehearsal. Teams that discover their config, their token or their EXCHANGE_HOST at 6:05pm spend the session reconnecting instead of trading, and the leaderboard does not wait.
Fix: rehearse against the headless sim and then a staging run that mirrors the live setup. Smoke-test the critical path — start, connect, one order, one fill, one score — before every session.
Crashing on ConnectionClosed
A socket blip is normal, not exceptional. A bot that lets the exception escape dies holding a position and becomes a spectator for the rest of the night. Nothing in your test suite noticed, because your tests never dropped the socket.
Fix: catch it, back off exponentially, reconnect, re-sync state from the exchange, resume. Then write the end-to-end test that kills the socket mid-session.
Retries that are not idempotent
The ack was slow, so the bot re-sent the order. Now it has two fills, twice the position it intended, and a P&L that disagrees with the exchange. The "five whys" chain ends at: there was no order id, so a replay was not a no-op.
Fix: tie every action to a client order id, make the handler idempotent, and reconcile against the exchange after any reconnect.
Unbounded queues under back-pressure
Messages arrive faster than on_tick processes them. The queue grows, latency grows with it, and your bot is soon trading on a market that existed thirty seconds ago — while memory climbs.
Fix: bound the queue and shed load deliberately. Dropping a stale book snapshot is correct; acting on it is not. Measure your per-tick budget and stay inside it.
Presenting the P&L instead of the process
"We made 14%" invites the one question you cannot answer: was that skill? Without the risk numbers and the reasoning behind each trade-off, a good result is indistinguishable from a lucky one — and the score is risk-adjusted anyway.
Fix: five acts, timed and rehearsed — problem, design, live run, results, next steps. Lead with the thesis, show the tearsheet, name the trade-offs, and say what you would fix with another week.
Check yourself

Three questions

Instant feedback, no grading.

Question 1
Your risk module and your router both pass all their unit tests, yet in the live session an order goes out that exceeds the position limit. What kind of bug is this?
C. This is the canonical integration failure: both speakers are correct and the conversation is not. Only a test that wires the two together — bot plus book, risk plus router — can see it. Test the conversation, not just the speakers.
Question 2
Mid-session your bot's position ledger says 240 lots of AAPL; the exchange says 300. What do you do first?
A. The venue's book is what settles and what the leaderboard scores; your ledger is a local cache of it. Adopt the exchange's number, re-derive your risk from it, then hunt the missed fill. Option C trades on a phantom position — that is how a reconciliation break becomes a real loss.
Question 3
Two traders both finish the season at +12% net worth. Team A's equity curve is smooth; team B's swings hard and had a 30% drawdown in week 6. How does exchange/scoring.py rank them?
B. ratio = excess / max(vol, VOL_FLOOR), then score = ratio × max(0, 1 − 2·mdd). B's higher volatility shrinks the ratio and its 30% drawdown cuts what is left by 60%. Same P&L, very different rank — the steadiest book wins, exactly as announced in Week 1.
