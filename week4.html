WEEK 4

Backtesting and Its Data

A backtest replays recorded market data through your strategy, simulates the fills and the costs, and ends in numbers you can be wrong about cheaply. This page follows what a backtest needs: the data and where it lives (a SQL database first, then kdb+ and the time-series databases, then plain Parquet files), the clock the replay must run on, and finally a Parquet-backed backtester whose P&L is identical at every replay speed until one call to time.time() breaks it.

Act I · Part 1

1What a backtest is, how it works, what it is for

A backtest answers one question: if this strategy had been running during that recording, what would it have done and what would it have earned? It never touches a market. It reads recorded events in the order they happened, hands each one to the strategy, turns the strategy's orders into simulated fills against the recorded book, charges the costs, keeps the books and, at the end, summarises the equity curve. Six stages, always the same ones, whether the tool is a 30-line script or a bank's research platform.

1
Data
Trades, quotes, book snapshots, events. Stored somewhere you can read fast (Parts 2–5).
2
Replay loop
Events in recorded order, one logical tick at a time (Part 6).
3
Strategy
Sees only the past and the present tick; returns orders.
4
Fills & fees
Orders reach the venue next tick, walk the recorded depth, pay the taker fee.
5
Accounting
Cash, positions, fees; mark to the mid every tick.
6
Metrics
P&L, drawdown, fills, in-sample vs out-of-sample, a digest of the fills.

The goal: falsify cheaply

A backtest is the cheapest place to kill an idea: minutes of CPU instead of a Friday of real P&L. If a strategy loses on the tape after honest costs, it will not win live. The reverse is not true. A positive backtest is an estimate under a fill model, a cost model and one path of history; it is never a proof. Treat it like a lab result: reproducible, with its assumptions written down, and compared against a baseline (doing nothing, buying and holding).

What a backtest cannot tell you

  • Your own impact. The recorded book never reacts to your orders; live, other bots do.
  • Your queue position. A resting order's fill depends on who was ahead of you, which the tape does not say.
  • A regime it never saw. One block of one Friday is one path; an edge that lived in it can die in the next.
  • Latency you did not model. Part 6: a 400 ms on_tick lands on a different book 44 % of the time right after the AAPL print.
  • The live-only bugs. Reconnects, rejects, a halted symbol, a slow socket.

The whole pipeline in a dozen lines, on four made-up ticks. The mid rises 0.6 % and the strategy still loses: it buys the ask one tick late, sells the bid one tick late, and pays 0.30 % on both legs.

Python · runs as shown, output pasted belowthe six stages in a dozen lines
# data -> replay -> strategy -> fills & fees -> accounting -> metrics
tape = [(1, 100.0, 100.2), (2, 100.4, 100.6), (3, 101.0, 101.2), (4, 100.6, 100.8)]  # (t, bid, ask)
fee, cash, pos, pending, curve = 0.003, 10_000.0, 0, [], []
for t, bid, ask in tape:                                  # replay on the logical clock
    for side, q in pending:                               # orders sent at t-1 fill now
        px = ask if side == "buy" else bid                # a buy lifts the ask
        sgn = 1 if side == "buy" else -1
        cash -= sgn * q * px + q * px * fee; pos += sgn * q
    pending = [("buy", 10)] if t == 1 else [("sell", 10)] if t == 3 else []  # strategy
    curve.append(cash + pos * (bid + ask) / 2)            # accounting: mark to the mid
dd = max(max(curve[: i + 1]) - e for i, e in enumerate(curve))
print(f"P&L {curve[-1] - 10_000:+.2f}  max drawdown {dd:.2f}  ticks {len(curve)}")
P&L -6.04  max drawdown 8.02  ticks 4

Act II · Part 2

2A backtest needs data: start with a relational database

The venue's recording is one JSON line per message: book snapshots, trades, acks, cancels, rejects, session events, leaderboards, all mixed together in one 265 MB file. To ask it anything you must parse all of it. The first instinct of most engineers is right: load it into a relational database, give each message type a table with typed columns, and let SQL do the searching.

SQLite or Postgres

  • SQLite is a library and a single file. No server, no users, one writer at a time. Perfect for one researcher and one tape: import sqlite3 and you have a database.
  • Postgres is a server: many concurrent readers and writers, roles and permissions, durability guarantees, replication. It is what a desk runs for orders, positions, accounts and reference data.
  • Both are row stores with B-tree indexes and the same SQL. That is their strength for transactions, and, as Part 3 shows, their weakness for tick history.

The schema: one table per message type

  • trades: one row per execution (seq, t, symbol, price, qty, aggressor).
  • quotes: top of book (bid, bid_qty, ask, ask_qty) per snapshot.
  • book_snapshots + book_levels: the depth is a list, which SQL normalises into a child table, one row per (snapshot, side, level). Ten levels a side turns one snapshot into twenty rows.
  • Keep both clocks and the recording order: t (the tick), seq (order of lines), and ts if you need wall time. Part 6 explains why.

Here is that schema in SQLite, with 200,000 synthetic quotes, and the one query every backtest asks: this symbol, this window of time. EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN shows what the database will do. Before the index it reads every row (SCAN); after a composite index on (symbol, t) it walks the B-tree straight to the first AAPL row at t390 and reads only the matching range (SEARCH). Order matters in a composite index: equality column first, range column second.

Interactive · full scan vs B-tree index

Same query, a growing table. Each square is a page of rows on disk. A full scan reads every page; an index reads one page per level of the tree, then only the leaf pages that hold the answer. The tree is shallow because each page holds hundreds of keys: depth grows with logfanout(rows). Writing is the other side of the trade: every insert must also update every index.

Pages on disk (one square ≈ —)
Measured on the Session 2 tape (the whole recording loaded into SQLite, one table per message type): database file 50,081,792 bytes, loaded in 4.6 s (3.6 s parsing JSON, 1.0 s inserting); four indexes add 1,794,048 bytes (+4 %). The index pays off on narrow questions: the AAPL event window at t400 drops from 10.4 ms to 0.02 ms. It does not help "AAPL mid at every tick" (3.7 ms without, 4.2 ms with), because that query reads every AAPL row anyway. Compare that question with the JSONL (264,960,858 bytes, 2.1–2.7 s) and, in Part 5, Parquet (6,740,873 bytes, 0.5–1.5 ms).

Act II · Part 3

3Why SQL is too limited for tick data

The index fixed the lookup. It did not fix the shape of the work. A backtest and most research queries read a few columns of many rows in time order, and they arrive as a firehose of appends. A relational row store was built for the opposite: read or update a whole row, by key, many times a second, with transactions. Five places it hurts:

1Row storage

Each row's columns sit together on the page. "AAPL mid at every tick" needs 2 of the book's 19 columns, and the database still reads every byte of every matching row, plus the rows of the other symbols that share its pages.

2Write throughput

Every insert writes the row, every index (one B-tree path each), and the log, inside a transaction. The arena tape has a median of 46 lines per tick and peaks at 269; a real feed is millions of messages a second. Bulk-load and drop indexes, or fall behind.

3Time-range scans

Research is "everything between t390 and t460", across all symbols, then aggregate. A B-tree finds the start fast; the scan itself is still row by row through pages full of columns you did not ask for.

4As-of joins are awkward

"The quote in force when each trade happened" has no operator in standard SQL. You write a correlated subquery or a LATERAL join: one index probe per trade at best, a nested loop at worst. It works, it reads badly, and it is easy to get subtly wrong.

5Size

No column compression, a per-row header, and a depth list exploded into twenty child rows per snapshot. The database is 50,081,792 bytes; the same tape in Parquet is 6,740,873.

Limit 4 in code: the as-of join in SQLite on seven real AAPL trades from t400, each matched to the last quote with seq at or before its own. Keep SQL where it belongs. Orders, fills you own, positions, accounts, reference data, the leaderboard: small, updated row by row, needing transactions. That is OLTP, and Postgres is excellent at it. Act II · Part 4

4Time-series databases: kdb+/q and its descendants

Banks and trading firms met these limits in the 1990s and answered with a different machine: kdb+, a column store with its own language, q. It is still the reference for tick data on most desks. Three ideas carry over to every tool after it, including the Parquet files you will build in the lab.

1Columns, not rows

A table is a set of equal-length vectors. "Average mid" reads the bid and ask vectors and nothing else, and a vector of floats compresses and streams through the CPU far better than mixed rows.

2Memory today, disk by date

A tickerplant logs every message and publishes it to a real-time database (today, in memory). At end of day it is written to the historical database: one directory per date, each table splayed into one file per column. A query on one date never opens the others.

3Time is a first-class key

Data is stored sorted by time within symbol (the `p# parted attribute on sym), so a window is a contiguous slice, and the as-of join aj is a built-in operator rather than a subquery.

Act II · Part 5

5A first look at Parquet

Parquet is an open file format that stores a table by column, cut into row groups, with statistics in the footer: kdb+'s column idea, in a file. DuckDB queries the files in place, with no load step and no server. It is also what the backtester in Part 7 reads.

Inside one file

  • Row groups: the table is cut into blocks of rows (4,096 in the lab's demo file).
  • Column chunks: inside a row group, each column is stored on its own, encoded and compressed.
  • Footer: the schema, and for every column chunk its min, max and null count. A reader opens the footer first.

How a query avoids work

  • Projection: only the columns named are read and decompressed.
  • Predicate pushdown: t BETWEEN 390 AND 460 is checked against each row group's min/max in the footer; groups that cannot match are never read. It only works because the file is sorted by tick.
  • Compression: neighbouring values in a column are alike. The trade table's symbol (10 values) drops from 36,388 bytes to 170 with a dictionary.

import os, tempfile
import pyarrow as pa, pyarrow.parquet as pq
n = 100_000                                        # 100k quotes, sorted by tick
tbl = pa.table({"t": [i // 35 for i in range(n)],
                "symbol": [("AAPL", "MSFT", "NVDA", "TSLA", "AMZN")[i % 5] for i in range(n)],
                "mid": [100 + (i % 997) / 100 for i in range(n)]})
path = os.path.join(tempfile.mkdtemp(), "quotes.parquet")
pq.write_table(tbl, path, row_group_size=10_000, compression="zstd")
md = pq.ParquetFile(path).metadata
stats = [md.row_group(g).column(0).statistics for g in range(md.num_row_groups)]
keep = [g for g, s in enumerate(stats) if not (s.max < 1000 or s.min > 1300)]
print(f"{md.num_row_groups} row groups; t in [1000, 1300] can only be in {keep}")
sym = md.row_group(0).column(1)
print(f"symbol column: {sym.encodings[-1]}, {sym.total_uncompressed_size / 10_000:.2f} bytes per value")
got = pq.read_table(path, columns=["t", "mid"], filters=[("t", ">=", 1000), ("t", "<=", 1300)])
print(f"read {got.num_rows:,} rows x {got.num_columns} of 3 columns")

Next, Act III. The tape is stored. Now the replay needs a clock. Is the time the machine reads the same as the time the market lived?

Act III · Part 6

6Logical time vs real time

Every line of the tape carries two clocks. Event time is when something happened in the market's own order of events. Processing time is when some machine got around to writing it down. Streaming systems make the same distinction (Flink, Kafka Streams, Beam). A backtest that confuses them is not a backtest of your strategy; it is a benchmark of your laptop.

Latency is a number of ticks

Give a live on_tick 400 ms to think. Over the whole block it lands on the book it decided on: 98 % of decisions see the same best ask. In the fifteen ticks after the AAPL print, 44 % land on a different best ask and one in ten on a mid 81 bp away. A replay with no latency fills on the decision book and invents that P&L. Hence the rule in Part 1's loop: an order decided at t reaches the venue during t + 1, and fills against the first book of that tick.

Wall clock only at the edges. Determinism means: same tape plus same code gives the same fills, on any machine, at any replay speed. That holds only if every decision (entry, exit, cooldown, "once a minute") reads the logical clock it is handed. The wall clock is fine at the edges, where it never changes what is decided: pacing a replay, socket timeouts, reconnect backoff, heartbeats, log lines.

Timeouts: three seconds of which clock?

The exit timer above is one kind of time-based decision. The most common one in a trading system is the timeout: “cancel the order if it has not filled in 3 seconds”, “re-quote after 500 ms”, “give up on the request after 2 s”. Live, 3 seconds means 3 seconds of market time because the market and the machine share one clock. In a replay they do not. If the timer reads time.time(), a replay at 5× makes “3 seconds” last 15 seconds of market time, and at full speed it never fires at all. The backtest then keeps orders alive far longer than the live bot would and books fills the live bot never gets. Run it slower than real time and the opposite happens: orders are cancelled too early. Either way the result depends on the machine.

The rule. A timeout inside the decision path is a scheduled event on the logical clock: put “fire at now + 3.0” in a queue and let the replay loop release it when the tape’s time passes it. Real timeouts stay real only where a real socket is involved (a connect timeout, a heartbeat).
Python · runs as shown, output pasted belowthe same 3 s timeout on two clocks
import heapq
class SimClock:
    """Logical time. A timer fires when the clock is advanced past it, never because the machine was slow."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.now, self._q, self._n = 0.0, [], 0
    def call_later(self, delay, fn):
        self._n += 1
        heapq.heappush(self._q, (self.now + delay, self._n, fn))
    def advance_to(self, t):                       # called once per tick with the tape's timestamp
        while self._q and self._q[0][0] <= t:
            due, _, fn = heapq.heappop(self._q)
            self.now = due
            fn()
        self.now = t

ts = [0.0, 1.0, 2.16, 3.35, 4.48, 5.48, 6.48, 7.58, 8.72, 9.87]
ts += [10.52, 11.68, 12.8, 13.86, 14.58, 15.78, 16.87, 17.97, 18.56, 19.58]

        alive[0] = False; log.append(f"cancelled by timer at t{400 + i}")
    clock.call_later(3.0, timeout)

How to keep time-based logic honest

  • Inject the clock. The strategy receives now (or a Clock object) and never calls time.time() or datetime.now() in a decision.
  • Schedule, do not sleep. call_later(3.0, cancel) puts an event in a heap keyed by logical time; the replay loop releases due events before it hands over the next tick.
  • asyncio: asyncio.sleep and wait_for count loop time, which is the wall clock. In a simulation, drive your own scheduler (or a loop with a fake clock) instead of awaiting real seconds.
  • Convert once, at the edge. A live bot’s “3 s” is a number of logical seconds; the connection to the venue is where real time legitimately appears.
  • Test it. Run the same tape at two speeds and assert the fills and the digest are identical. If they differ, something in a decision path reads the machine’s clock.

Look-ahead is a clock violation

The classic bug, sig[t] * ret[t], decides at t with the close of t and is paid the move into t. In clock terms: a decision at logical time t read data stamped after it. In a vectorised backtest it is one missing shift(1):

The list, and what to do about each one

mistakewhat it looks likewhy it happenshow to avoid it
1 · Look-ahead biasA Sharpe far too good for a simple rule (2 to 5 on one instrument, much higher on a diversified book); the curve is smooth and the drawdowns are tiny. A decision at time t uses data stamped after t: today’s close, a revised figure, tomorrow’s open, today’s index membership. Lag every feature by one bar (shift(1)). Prefer an event-driven loop that only hands the strategy the market as of now. For each input name the moment it became knowable. Test: delay the signal one more bar; a real edge degrades a little, a leak collapses.
2 · Free tradingEdge exists but disappears live; the more trades, the bigger the gap. Fills at the last price ignore the spread, the fee, the queue and the depth; every round trip pays them twice. A buy lifts the ask, a sell hits the bid, a large order walks the book. Charge the venue’s fees. Report net, plus turnover, and re-run at double the cost: if the edge dies, it was never there.
3 · Survivorship bias“Buy the dip” works on every stock, forever. The names that went bankrupt, were delisted or acquired are missing from the data you loaded. Use a dataset with dead tickers, each present only for the dates it traded, and with the delisting return included. Build the universe as it was known on each date.
4 · Tuning and testing on the same dataA large gap between the first (tuned) half and the second half; the best parameter moves when you shift the window. With 40 settings you keep the luckiest draw, which fits the noise of that sample. Few parameters with an economic reason. Choose on an earlier period, then test once on a later one you never looked at; roll it forward (walk-forward). Count every variant you tried and raise the bar accordingly.
5 · Cherry-picked windowOne impressive stretch presented as the whole story. Any strategy has a best quarter; choosing the dates after seeing the result is selection bias. Fix the period, the symbols and the metrics before you run. Report the whole sample and the worst stretch, not the best.
6 · One regime, one pathGreat average, awful in one year; works only in one volatility regime. Markets change; a long average hides that the edge lived in one window. One historical path is one draw. Break the P&L down by period and by regime; add a stress period; size for a regime you have not seen. (Not in the lab above: it needs real history.)

Before you believe a curve

  • Can I name the moment each input became knowable? (look-ahead)
  • Is the P&L net of fees, spread and depth, and does it survive twice the cost? (free trading)
  • Is every name that existed on that date in the data, including the ones that died? (survivorship)
  • Did I choose the parameters on data I then tested on, and how many did I try? (tuning)
  • Did I choose the dates before seeing the result, and do I show the worst stretch? (cherry-picking, regimes)
  • Would a baseline (hold, do nothing) do about as well? A result is only meaningful next to its baselines.

The link with the job

Why this matters on a trading desk

On a desk the research loop is only as fast as the question you can ask of the history. If every question means parsing a quarter of a gigabyte of JSON, you ask three a day and stop exploring; if it costs ten milliseconds, you ask a thousand and actually test the idea. That is why tick data lives in column stores, kdb+ on most bank and HFT desks and Parquet files queried in place nearly everywhere else, while orders, positions and accounts stay in a relational database where transactions matter. Choosing the store by the shape of the work is a decision you will be asked to defend.

The as-of join is the most used and most abused operation in market-data research. Every fill analysis, every markout and every transaction-cost report matches events to the quote in force, and the key and direction decide whether the answer is honest. On the Session 2 tape, joining on the tick alone hands every AAPL trade in tick 400 a one-sided book recorded after it traded; joining forward is exactly right for a post-trade markout and exactly wrong inside a backtest. Write the definition down (the lab's book_eot) and make every engine use it.

Determinism is what turns a backtest into evidence. If the same tape and the same code can produce two P&Ls, a parameter search is measuring your machine, and the fastest replay, the one you run a thousand times, is the one that lies most: on the AAPL slice the wall-clock exit made +902 at full speed against +505 for the honest run. Desks enforce this by injecting the clock into the strategy, replaying recorded production days through the live code path, and comparing fill digests in CI, so that any difference between replay and live is a real difference and not an accident of timing.

Costs and latency are positions you hold whether you model them or not. At the arena's 0.30 % taker fee, momentum lost 16,501 over the block through 1,187 fills, and the in-sample tuning chose the lookback that lost most out of sample. A latency of one tick decided whether event_ride caught the 36 shares offered at 197.18 during the AAPL ramp or found an empty ask side. Report P&L net of fees against baselines, count the trials you ran, and treat latency as a number of ticks you measure, because your backtest will otherwise invent exactly the trades that pay.

Interview

Interview questions

8 questions a quant-developer interviewer could ask about this week: 2 warm-up, 4 core, 2 senior. Answer out loud first, then open the model answer.

warm-upWhat is a backtest for, and what can it not tell you?

It is a cheap way to falsify a strategy: replay recorded events in order, let the strategy see only the past, simulate fills and costs, and see whether it still makes money against a baseline. A loss after honest costs is strong evidence; a profit is only an estimate under the fill model, the cost model and one path of history. It cannot show your own market impact, because the recorded book never reacts to you, nor your queue position, a regime missing from the data, unmodelled latency, or the failures that only happen live, such as reconnects and rejects.

Related skill: The backtest pipeline and its goal
warm-upRow store or column store for five years of quotes, and why?

A column store. The workload is append-only and reads a few columns of many rows in time order, such as the bid and ask of one symbol over a window. A column store reads only those columns, compresses each one well because the values are similar, and keeps data sorted by time within symbol so a window is a contiguous slice. A row store reads every column of every matching row and pays an index update on every insert. Orders, positions and accounts are the opposite workload, small and transactional, and belong in a relational row store such as Postgres.

Related skill: Column stores & time-series databases
coreDesign a SQL schema for trades, quotes and book snapshots. Which indexes, and what hurts first as it grows?

One table per message type with typed columns: trades (seq, t, symbol, price, qty, aggressor), quotes (seq, t, symbol, bid, bid_qty, ask, ask_qty), and book snapshots with the depth in a child table keyed by (snapshot, side, level). Keep the event clock, the recording order and, separately, the wall-clock timestamp. Index (symbol, t), equality column first and range second, and check with the query plan that it searches rather than scans. What hurts first is the write path, since every insert updates every index, then the size of the exploded depth table and the cost of as-of joins written as correlated subqueries.

Related skill: Tick data in a relational database
coreWhat is an as-of join? Write it two ways and name a way it silently goes wrong.

For each row on the left, take the last row on the right whose key is at or before it, usually by symbol: each trade gets the quote in force when it happened. In pandas it is merge_asof(trades, quotes, on='time', by='symbol', direction='backward'); in DuckDB, trades ASOF JOIN quotes ON trades.symbol = quotes.symbol AND trades.time >= quotes.time; in q, aj[`sym`time; trade; quote]. It goes wrong silently when the key is too coarse, so several quotes share a timestamp and an arbitrary one wins, when inputs are unsorted or the key dtypes differ, or when someone uses a forward match inside a backtest, which is look-ahead.

Related skill: The as-of join
coreYour Parquet query filters on time and is still slow. What do you check?

First whether the files are sorted by the filter column: predicate pushdown skips a row group only if its min/max statistics exclude the range, and a file sorted by symbol has every row group spanning the whole day. Then whether the query names only the columns it needs, since projection is the other half of the saving. Then row-group size, since huge groups prune coarsely and tiny ones bloat the footer. Finally read the plan with EXPLAIN in DuckDB to see whether the filter and the projection were actually pushed into the Parquet scan.

Related skill: Parquet: columns, row groups & predicate pushdown
coreHow do you model fills and latency for a strategy that trades on scheduled news?

Fill at the next tick, not the decision tick, against the book recorded at that moment, walking the depth up to the limit price and dropping or resting the remainder by order type; charge the taker fee on the notional. Treat latency as an integer number of ticks and test several values, because on a thin book around an announcement the result is lumpy: on the AAPL earnings ramp an order arriving one tick after the decision filled 36 shares, while zero or two ticks found no ask under the cap. Expect one-sided books and empty levels exactly when the news hits, and never assume the mid.

Related skill: Fill simulation, costs & latency
seniorThe same backtest on the same data gives a different P&L on every run. How do you find the cause?

Make the run reproducible enough to compare: record a digest of the fill list, rerun at two replay speeds and on two machines, and find the first fill that differs. The usual cause is a decision that reads the machine rather than the data: time.time(), datetime.now() or a sleep-based cooldown, so its length in market time depends on CPU speed. Others are unseeded randomness, iteration over an unordered container, threads racing on shared state, or parallel workers merging results in completion order. Fix it by handing the strategy the logical time and an injected clock, and keep the wall clock at the edges only.

Related skill: A deterministic replay on the logical clock
seniorA researcher shows you Sharpe 3 on tick data. How do you try to break it?

Look for leaks first, because they are cheap to find: lag the signal by one tick and see whether the edge degrades a little or collapses, check every as-of join for its key and direction, and check that nothing reads restated or future reference data. Then the fill model: next-tick fills against recorded depth, the real fee schedule, latency in ticks, one-sided books. Then compare with baselines and count the trials behind the chosen parameters, and look at the out-of-sample block they never touched. Finally replay at two speeds and confirm the same fill digest, to rule out a wall-clock dependency.
