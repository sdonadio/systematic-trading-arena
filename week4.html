Backtester Architecture

A backtest is the cheapest place to fail and the easiest place to fool yourself — this week you build one that does the first and refuses the second.

  • You will be able to replay your Week 1–3 bot offline through SimSession and the same matching engine that fills you live.
  • You will be able to report honest metrics — Sharpe, max drawdown, and the risk-adjusted score — net of fees and slippage, not gross.
  • You will be able to audit an on_tick for look-ahead bias and defend every assumption your fill model makes.

What is it?

A controlled experiment on data your strategy has never traded

A backtest simulates a strategy on historical data: replay the past, let the strategy decide, tally the P&L. Framed properly it is a hypothesis test. You have an idea about the market; the backtest asks whether that idea would have made money before a dollar is at risk.

It is the cheapest place to fail. A bug or a bad edge caught in simulation costs you seconds. Caught live, it costs your P&L and your rank. It is also the easiest place to fool yourself: most published backtests are wrong, and the failure is almost never the arithmetic. Half of this week is the mechanics. The other half is the biases that make a beautiful curve worthless.

It IS

A controlled experiment — fix the data, fix the rules, measure the behaviour repeatably. And a debugger for logic and risk: sizing, stops, and edge cases surface before they cost money.

It ISN'T

A promise. Past performance is a sample, not the population. And it is not the live market: no queue, no latency, no adverse selection unless you model them. A backtest is only as honest as its assumptions.

Two architectures

Vectorised computes signals over a whole price series with numpy and pandas at once. It is fast to write and perfect for a first screen — but it makes peeking at the future a one-character mistake, and it cannot express fills, partial orders, or an order book. Event-driven steps through time one event at a time, so the strategy only ever sees the past. It costs more code and more seconds, and it mirrors the live loop exactly. AlgoArena's simulator is event-driven for that reason.

Rule of thumb. Prototype vectorised for speed, but validate — and go live — event-driven. If the two disagree, trust neither until you know why.

for event in market_data:          # 1. next market event

    signal = strategy.on_tick(     # 2. strategy sees ONLY
        market, portfolio)         #    data up to 'now'
    if signal:
        order = to_order(signal)   # 3. turn signal -> order
        fills = book.match(order)  # 4. simulate the fill
        for f in fills:
            portfolio.apply(f)     # 5. update cash + position
    equity.append(portfolio.net_worth())
How does it work?

The fill model is the backtest

Everything upstream of the fill is arithmetic you control. The fill is where your simulation meets a market that does not care what price you wanted. Slippage is the gap: execution price minus decision price. It comes from the spread you cross, your position in the queue, and the market moving while you act.

Optimistic — and wrong

fill = last_price
# you never crossed anything.
# free liquidity, infinite size,
# zero queue. This is fantasy.

Honest

fill = ask if side=='buy' else bid
# large order: walk the levels
fill = vwap(book, side, qty)
slip = fill - mid    # measure it

A buy lifts the ask; a sell hits the bid. A large order walks the book to progressively worse levels, so its average fill is a volume-weighted price, not a touch price. On top of that sit the costs the venue charges you and the cost you inflict on yourself.

Explicit costs

Commissions, exchange fees, the maker/taker split. In AlgoArena the taker pays 0.0015 of notional and the maker earns a 0.0010 rebate. The fee model is part of your P&L, not an afterthought.

Implicit costs

The bid–ask spread you cross on every round trip. A tight-margin, high-turnover strategy dies here first — and it dies quietly, because the gross curve still looks fine.

Market impact

Your own order moves the price against you. Impact grows with size and shrinks with liquidity; the standard first approximation is proportional to sqrt(size).

The turnover trap. Halve your holding period and you roughly double your cost drag. Always report P&L net of costs, never gross — and know your breakeven edge in basis points before you tune anything else.

Why does it matter in trading?

Because the flattered curve is the one you would have traded

One number never tells the story. Read return, risk, and the shape of the drawdowns together, then ask what the backtest had to assume to produce them.

Return & risk

CAGR — compound annual growth rate, return normalised to a year. Sharpe — mean excess return divided by standard deviation: return per unit of volatility. Max drawdown — the largest peak-to-trough drop, the pain you must survive to earn the return.

Risk-adjusted & shape

Calmar — CAGR divided by max drawdown: reward per unit of worst case. Profit factor — gross profit over gross loss. Watch hit rate, win/loss size, turnover and time-under-water too. A high Sharpe with a 60% drawdown is untradeable.

Backtest for the score, not the headline. exchange/scoring.py ranks you on return divided by volatility and then applies a drawdown penalty — not raw final net worth. A high-vol, deep-drawdown curve scores badly even when it ends up.

Look-ahead bias — the cardinal sin

honest — signal lagged one bar peeking — decides on today's close max drawdown of the selected curve

Wrong

# today's signal uses today's close,
# but you 'decided' before it existed
df['sig'] = df['close'] > roll_mean
df['ret'] = df['sig'] * df['close'].pct_change()

Right

# act on what you actually knew
df['sig'] = (df['close'] > roll_mean).shift(1)
df['ret'] = df['sig'] * df['close'].pct_change()
Same 220 bars, same rule — long when the close is above its 20-bar mean, short otherwise. The only difference is when the signal is allowed to act. Because today's close is inside today's return, the peeking version is quietly reading the answer off the back of the page. An event-driven loop makes this structurally hard: the strategy is only ever handed the past.

Common mistakes

Five ways a backtest flatters you

None of these are arithmetic errors. Every one of them produces a curve that is internally consistent, plausible, and wrong. Learn to recognise the shape of each.

Look-ahead bias — using information that did not exist at decision time

The classic form is a signal computed from the same bar it trades on: df['sig'] = df['close'] > roll_mean then multiplied by that bar's return. Any peek at the future — a close, a high, tomorrow's data, a revised earnings figure — inflates results, often past the point of absurdity. Reference data leaks too: today's index membership, today's sector map, today's credit rating applied to a 2019 backtest.

The fix. Lag every signal by at least one bar (.shift(1)), and prefer an event-driven loop where the strategy is structurally incapable of seeing past “now”. Then audit: for each input to on_tick, name the moment it became knowable.

Survivorship bias — testing only on the names that made it

Backtest on today's index members and you have quietly excluded every company that went bankrupt, delisted, or was acquired. You are testing the winners only. A “buy the dip” rule looks brilliant when every stock in the sample eventually recovered — because the ones that did not were deleted from the data before you ever loaded it.

The fix. Use a survivorship-bias-free dataset that includes dead tickers, with each name present only for the dates it actually traded. Its cousin is selection bias: cherry-picking the symbols, dates, or markets where the strategy happens to work.

Data snooping and overfitting — the luckiest random draw

Tune enough parameters and you fit the noise, not the signal: the backtest is perfect and the future is a disaster. Try 500 variants and keep the best, and that winner is a draw from the right tail of a distribution of random results, not an edge. The tell is a large in-sample / out-of-sample gap, and a “best” parameter that moves every time you shift the window.

The fix. Prefer few parameters, an economic rationale, and robustness to small changes over a fragile optimum. Count your trials honestly — the more configurations you test, the higher the bar for significance. And reserve data you never touch.

Assuming perfect fills — free liquidity at the mid

fill = last_price is optimistic on three axes at once: it ignores the spread you cross, the queue you sit in, and the fact that 10,000 shares do not appear at one price. High turnover multiplies the error — every round trip pays it twice — which is why tight-margin strategies are the ones that die on contact with a realistic cost model.

The fix. A buy lifts the ask, a sell hits the bid, and a large order walks the levels to a volume-weighted price. Add the venue's fee schedule, add impact, and report net. Then be pessimistic: a backtest that survives conservative assumptions is one you can trust with capital.

Ignoring regime change — one long average over several different markets

Markets are non-stationary. Volatility regimes, rate cycles, liquidity droughts and structural breaks shift the data-generating process, and yesterday's relationship stops holding. A single long backtest averages over regimes and hides that the edge lived in exactly one of them; the blended Sharpe flatters a fragile strategy.

The fix. Break P&L out by period and by regime. A robust strategy is merely acceptable everywhere; a fragile one is spectacular in one window and awful elsewhere. And accept the humility: no backtest covers a regime it never saw, so size positions for the one you have not yet lived through.
