Part 0 · Why machine learning in trading

ML sharpens the signal, never the system

Why this part exists. Weeks 1–7 built a bot that runs: a gateway, a book, a signal, orders, an OMS, fast data. This week adds machine learning to that bot, so the first question is where it goes and what it is allowed to change. The answer sets the rule for every later part: a model is kept only if the strategy gets better. Part 1 then asks what kind of learning fits the question you have.

A trading bot is a pipeline: ticks come in through the gateway, update the book, feed a signal, become orders, and are tracked by the OMS. Machine learning swaps one or two boxes — the signal, sometimes the execution model — and leaves the plumbing, the risk checks and the fees exactly where they were. That is why it can make a strategy better, and why it can only be judged by the same P&L after fees as the rule it replaces.

Remember

ML replaces or sharpens one box, the signal and sometimes the execution model, never the system around it. A model is judged on the same P&L after fees as the rule it replaces.

In your bot: the model lives behind signal(); everything else in your trader stays as it is.

What you are looking at

Try this

  1. Start at 53% hit rate. Read the callout: 1.3σ from a coin, and the line stays inside the grey band.
  2. Drag Signal-to-noise to 58%. Now it is 3.6σ; the backtest climbs out of the band to +116.

What to notice

A 52–53% hit rate over 500 trades is 0.9–1.3σ from a coin: you cannot tell it from luck. Even a real 58% edge loses most of its value live if the market drifts away from the training period (+116 becomes +28), and turns negative when your own trading moves the price against you (−76).

Remember

Three reasons ML is dangerous in trading: the signal-to-noise ratio is low, markets are not stationary so models rot, and your trades feed back into prices. Budget as much time for catching yourself cheating as for fitting.

In your bot: count your trades before you trust a hit rate, and watch it live (Part 7).

Name the three reasons ML is dangerous in trading.

Low signal-to-noise (small edges hide in luck), non-stationarity (the market drifts away from the training data, so models rot), and feedback (your own trades move the price and erode the edge).

Part 1 · Supervised vs unsupervised

Labels or no labels

Why this part exists. Part 0 put the model inside the signal box. Before you build one, you have to say what question it answers. If you have an answer for every past row (did the price move, did the order fill), you learn to predict it: supervised. If you only have the states, you learn their structure: unsupervised. Part 2 then builds the columns of X that both kinds of model eat.

Supervised learning needs an answer for every row: features X known at time t, a label y about t + h, and a model that maps one to the other — a class (up/down, fill/no fill) or a number (return, cost). Unsupervised learning has only X and looks for structure: regimes, common factors, the one point that does not belong. Same dots below, two different questions.

What to notice

The classifier needs the answers; k-means never sees them and still finds three groups (calm, trending, stressed). Raising k always lowers the within-cluster distance (57% down at k = 3, 71% at k = 5), so that number cannot pick k; the elbow bends at k = 3. PC1 explains 88% of the variance because vol and spread rise together. The ringed dots sit far from every centre: a wide quote in a calm market is a broken bot, not a regime.

The 82% is in-sample: it is measured on the dots the line was fitted on.

Remember

Supervised learning maps X at time t to a label y about t + h, either a class or a number. Unsupervised learning has only X and finds structure in it: clusters (regimes), factors (PCA) and outliers (anomalies).

In your bot: a supervised model gives a signal; a regime cluster or an anomaly flag decides which rule trades, or whether to stop.

Part 2 · Technical indicators

The feature factory, and why the parameters explode

Why this part exists. Part 1 said a supervised model maps features X at time t to a label about t + h. This part is about X: the columns you build from prices and the book. Indicators are the classical features, and each one hides windows and thresholds you choose. Count them honestly and you see why Part 4 (too many dimensions) and Part 5 (overfitting) are coming, and why a better strategy starts with fewer, chosen features.

Indicators are the classical, hand-made features: averages, momentum, RSI, bands, VWAP distance, book imbalance. Every one of them has a window and a threshold, and every window is a parameter you chose. Multiply by symbols and by the model's own knobs and you are fitting thousands of numbers to a few hundred independent observations — the door to Parts 4 and 5.

The indicator panel on a live price path

Simulated, seeded path (one symbol). Every slider you touched is one more number the backtest was fitted with — the counter in the top-right corner is already counting them.

What you are looking at

One simulated price path scrolls to the left. The lines on the price are moving averages (SMA, EMA), Bollinger bands and VWAP; the two strips underneath are RSI and order-book imbalance. The housing on the right shows the value of each indicator at t, the number your on_tick would see now. KNOBS SET counts the parameters you chose.

Try this

  1. Leave the defaults (SMA, EMA, RSI, book imbalance). The label above the buttons reads 7 parameters chosen, 1 symbol.
  2. Turn on Bollinger and VWAP. The count goes to 10: Bollinger alone adds a window and a width.
  3. Press Pause, then drag SMA window from 20 to 5 and then to 120. Watch the line go from hugging the price to lagging far behind it.
  4. Drag Bollinger width k from 2.0σ to 1.0σ. The price touches the bands far more often.
  5. Drag RSI window from 14 to 3. RSI swings between its extremes; at 50 it barely leaves the middle.

What to notice

The same price gives a different feature for every window you pick. A short SMA is noisy and reacts fast; a long one is smooth and late. The band width decides how often a “band touch” signal fires. None of these choices is given by the market: each one is a number you fitted, even if you fitted it by eye. Ten knobs on one symbol is already ten.

Remember

An indicator is a hand-made feature with parameters: a window, and usually a threshold. Every parameter you pick by looking at the backtest is a parameter you fitted.

In your bot: write down every window and threshold you use, and count them.

The feature factory — and its odometer

add indicators, windows, thresholds, symbols, a model
 
Independent observations assumed: 2,000 non-overlapping labels. “Luck” is the expected best annualised Sharpe among that many zero-edge trials, √(2 ln trials) — what a search finds when there is nothing to find.

What you are looking at

The hopper on the left holds the indicators. Each one drops into a machine whose three gears multiply it by the windows, the thresholds and the symbols you try. Column names like rsi_14·NVDA come out on the belt. Three odometers count feature columns, the parameters of the model on top, and the trials a search would run. The gauge compares columns with the 2,000 independent observations assumed; LUCK is the Sharpe a search finds when there is nothing to find.

Try this

  1. Read the defaults in the callout: 1,800 columns = 10 indicators × 20 windows × 9 symbols, 0.90 per independent observation, 27,000 trials, luck Sharpe near 4.5.
  2. Switch Model on top from Logistic to MLP. Trials go to 291,600 and the luck Sharpe to 5.0; the model-parameters odometer jumps from 1,801 to about 29,000.
  3. Set the model back to Logistic. Drag Windows per indicator to 30, Thresholds per rule to 12 and Symbols to 12: 3,600 columns, 1.80 per observation, 216,000 trials.
  4. Now go small: switch off all indicators but three in the hopper, then set 5 windows, 2 thresholds, 1 symbol. The callout reads 15 columns, 150 trials, luck near 3.2.

What to notice

The counts multiply, they do not add: one extra symbol or one extra window multiplies every column. At the defaults you have almost one column per independent observation, so a model can fit almost anything. The luck number grows slowly, as √(2 ln trials), but it never stops: with 27,000 trials a strategy with no edge is expected to show a Sharpe near 4.5 in the search. Even 150 trials buy a luck Sharpe of 3.2.

Remember

Indicators × windows × thresholds × symbols, plus the model's own parameters and its hyperparameter grid, quickly gives more columns than independent observations. The best result of a large search is mostly luck unless it survives data it never saw.

In your bot: keep the number of features and trials small, and report how many you tried, not just the best one.

Inside on_tick: add the new, drop the old

rolling window · O(n) vs O(1)
Week 6, applied: keep a running sum (and sum of squares) and a deque; SMA, Bollinger, VWAP, realised vol all update in two operations per tick, whatever the window.

What you are looking at

A row of prices, with a bracket marking the last n of them: the rolling window. Each tick a new price enters with a + and the oldest leaves with a −. Two lanes count the work: RECOMPUTE sums all n values again each tick; RUNNING SUM keeps s and does s += new − old.

Try this

  1. Leave the window at 12 and press Step one tick ten times. Recompute spends 12 operations per tick, the running sum 2.
  2. Drag Window n to 30 (the counters reset). Step again: recompute now costs 30 per tick, the running sum still 2.
  3. Drag the window to 3 and compare: 3 against 2. The gain only shows on long windows.

What to notice

Both lanes produce the same moving average. The cost of recomputing grows with the window; the running sum costs 2 operations per tick whatever n is. With 1,800 columns, many of them on long windows, that difference decides whether on_tick fits in its budget (Part 7).

Remember

A rolling mean, variance, VWAP or realized vol can be updated in O(1) per tick: keep a running sum (and a sum of squares) and a deque, add the new value, drop the old one.

In your bot: compute features incrementally in on_tick; never recompute a whole window.

What this changes in your bot: fewer, chosen features computed in O(1) inside on_tick — every extra column must earn its place in a better strategy, out-of-sample, after fees.

Part 3 · The data / ML pipeline

Nine stations, and a leak at every one

Why this part exists. Part 2 built the features. This part runs them through the whole pipeline, from the hypothesis to the number you report. Each station can let the future into the past, and a leak does not look like a bug: it looks like a great model that loses on Friday. Once the pipeline is honest, Part 4 asks how many features that honest pipeline can afford.

Hypothesis first, then data, cleaning, scaling, merging, splitting, training, one look at the test set, and an evaluation in the units the leaderboard pays. Every station can quietly let the future in, and a leak never looks like a bug: it looks like a great model. The machine below starts with five leaks switched on — find them and fix them.

The live number never moves: it is what a market with no edge pays, 51%. Every point the backtest shows above it came from the future. The scores are illustrative weights; the failures are the real ones.

Remember

A leak makes a backtest look better and live trading no better. Check every station: hypothesis first, clean, scale on train only, merge as-of backward, split in time with purge and embargo, tune on validation, open the test set once, evaluate in P&L after fees.

In your bot: when the backtest is much better than live, look for a leak before you look for a better model.

Remember

Pick metrics that match the decision: classification metrics for the probabilities, regression metrics for the size of the move, and P&L after costs, Sharpe, turnover and drawdown against a baseline for the strategy.

In your bot: report every model against always-up and your momentum rule, after fees, with its turnover.

The leakage gap: shuffled K-fold vs time on a random walk

real k-NN · 1,000 ticks · nothing to learn
 
Features: past returns over 10, 20 and 40 ticks; label: sign of the next h ticks; model: 7-nearest neighbours. Neighbouring rows share almost all of both, so a shuffle puts the answer in the training set. Set h = 1 and the gap closes.

What you are looking at

A random walk of 1,000 ticks: there is nothing to predict. The same 7-nearest-neighbour model is scored three ways. The coloured strips show which rows each scheme puts in training (blue) and test (amber): shuffled 5-fold mixes them, the time split cuts at 70%, and walk-forward moves forward with an embargo gap. The bars are the three accuracies; the dots are earlier walks.

Try this

  1. Read the default walk (h = 20): shuffled K-fold 63.9%, time split 51.0%, walk-forward 50.0%. A 13-point gap.
  2. Press Re-draw the walk four times. Shuffled stays between about 60% and 67%; the time-based scores stay near 46–55%.
  3. Drag Label horizon h to 40: shuffled reaches 69.3%, a 15-point gap.
  4. Drag it to 1: shuffled 52.6%, time split 50.7%, walk-forward 49.2%. The gap closes.

What to notice

The walk has no edge, so any honest score is about 50%. Neighbouring rows share almost the same rolling features and almost the same forward label; a shuffle puts the neighbour of every test row in the training set, and k-NN simply looks it up. The longer the label horizon, the more the labels overlap and the bigger the fake edge. With h = 1 there is little overlap and the gap disappears.

Remember

Random K-fold lies on time series: overlapping features and labels leak across the split. Use a time split or walk-forward, and purge or embargo at least the label horizon between training and test.

In your bot: validate with TimeSeriesSplit(gap=h) or walk-forward, never a shuffled split.

What this changes in your bot: only a number earned through this pipeline — scaled on train, merged as-of, walk-forward, test touched once, P&L after fees beating the baseline — gets to change the strategy.

Part 4 · The curse of dimensionality

More columns, emptier space

Why this part exists. Part 2 showed how fast the columns multiply; Part 3 built a pipeline that measures a model honestly. This part explains why many columns hurt even in an honest pipeline: the data gets thin in every direction. It gives you three tools to cut the columns back — shrinkage, PCA and LDA — so the model learns signal, not noise. Part 5 then shows the same problem from the model side: overfitting.

Every indicator column from Part 2 is one more dimension. In high dimensions the volume moves to the skin, every point is about as far from every other, and the data needed to fill the space grows exponentially — while a season of arena history stays small and noisy. Three remedies: shrink the coefficients, rotate to the few directions that carry the variance (PCA), or project onto the direction that separates the classes (LDA).

What to notice

At 10 columns most of the volume is already at the edge, and the nearest point is 0.37 of the farthest one, so “close” stops meaning much. The rows needed grow by ten for every column you add: 1e11 at d = 10 is far more than any arena tape. The data does not change; the space around it gets empty.

Remember

In high dimensions the volume sits on the skin, distances concentrate (nearest and farthest become alike) and the sample needed grows exponentially with the number of features. With a small, noisy sample, every extra column costs you.

In your bot: keep the feature list short, and be most careful with models that rely on distances (k-NN, RBF kernels).

Shrinkage: pull the coefficients in

8 features · 3 matter · 40 rows
 
Ridge shrinks every coefficient and shares the weight between correlated twins; lasso sets some exactly to zero (feature selection); elastic net does both. Choose λ on later data, never on the test set.

What you are looking at

Eight bars are the eight coefficients of a linear model fitted on 40 rows; only three features really matter and they come in correlated pairs. White ticks are the true values, the dashed ghost is the fit with no penalty, and a lamp goes out when a coefficient hits exactly zero. The two charts below are the coefficient paths across λ and the error on 400 later rows.

Try this

  1. Keep Ridge · L2 and drag Penalty λ from 0.001 to 0.316: all bars shrink, all 8 stay non-zero.
  2. Read the callout: MSE on later rows goes from 5.51 (barely penalised) to a best of 5.45 at λ = 0.049.
  3. Drag λ to 3.2: MSE jumps to 8.88 — the real signal is being shrunk too.
  4. Click Lasso · L1 and set λ = 0.316: only 5 of 8 coefficients are left; lamps go out.
  5. At λ = 3.2 lasso keeps 0 of 8. Then try Elastic net at 0.316: 6 of 8 survive.

What to notice

A small penalty helps on new data (ridge 5.51 → 5.45, lasso best 5.23 at λ = 0.115) because it stops the model from chasing noise in 40 rows. Ridge spreads weight across correlated twins and never zeroes them; lasso picks one and drops the rest. Too much penalty kills the signal: MSE climbs to 12.83, the error of predicting nothing.

Remember

Regularisation trades a little bias for less variance. Ridge (L2) shrinks every coefficient; lasso (L1) sets some exactly to zero, so it also selects features; elastic net mixes both. λ is chosen on later data, never on the test set.

In your bot: fit your logistic or linear model with a penalty, and choose its strength by walk-forward validation.

PCA vs LDA: the direction of most variance is not the direction that separates

3 market states · 2 features
 
PCA is feature extraction without labels: it keeps variance, which may be noise. LDA is supervised: it keeps separation. Lasso, by contrast, is feature selection: it keeps original columns.

The direction with the most variance is not the direction that tells the classes apart. PCA never looks at the labels, so it keeps the spread (79%) and loses the separation (40%). LDA uses the labels and keeps one number that still separates the classes (90%).

Remember

PCA is unsupervised feature extraction: it keeps the directions of largest variance, which may be noise. LDA is supervised: it keeps the directions that separate the classes. Lasso, by contrast, selects original columns.

In your bot: if you compress features with PCA, check on later data that the components still predict your label.

You add 30 indicator columns to a k-nearest-neighbour model trained on one season of ticks. Why does it get worse?

With 30 dimensions the points are almost equally far from each other, so the “nearest” neighbours are not really near, and one season is far too small to fill the space. The extra columns add distance noise, not information.

Ridge or lasso: which one can drop a feature completely, and why does that matter?

Lasso (L1): its penalty sets some coefficients exactly to zero, so it also selects features. Ridge (L2) only shrinks them. A model with fewer features is cheaper in on_tick and easier to check.

PCA finds an axis with 79% of the variance. Why can it still be useless for predicting the regime?

PCA ignores the labels. The direction with the most variance can be noise or a factor that has nothing to do with the class; LDA, which uses the labels, can separate the classes on a direction with less variance.

Part 5 · Linear models and their limits

Watch a model memorise the noise

Why this part exists. Part 4 showed that thin data cannot support many columns. This part shows the same thing inside one model: give a linear model more flexibility and it starts to learn the noise. Linear and logistic regression are the first rung of the ladder and the baseline every later model must beat. Part 6 then climbs to non-linear models, with the same overfitting knob on each.

Linear models are the first rung: least squares has a closed form, t-stats and an R² you can explain, and logistic regression turns a score into a probability you can size a position with. Their knob is flexibility. Drag the degree: error on the training points always falls, error on new points turns around — that U is overfitting, and with market-grade noise it arrives early.

  2. Drag Decision threshold τ to 0.30: 196 buys, recall 93%, precision 57%.
  3. Drag to 0.70: 41 buys, precision 78%, recall 27%.

Moving τ only trades precision for recall; it does not create edge. Remember

Logistic regression outputs a probability through a sigmoid and is fitted by minimising log-loss. The threshold sets the precision/recall trade-off; calibration decides whether the probability can be used for sizing.

In your bot: size positions with a calibrated probability, and check calibration on later data before you trust it.

Training RMSE keeps falling from degree 1 to degree 15. Which degree do you keep?

The one where error on later data is lowest (degree 3 here, test RMSE 0.357), or the simplest one close to it. Training error always falls with more flexibility, so it cannot choose the degree.

You move the threshold from 0.5 to 0.7. Precision goes up. Did the model get better?

Part 6 · Non-linear models

Same data, eight boundaries

Why this part exists. Part 5 ended on data a straight line cannot separate: XOR stays near 50–60% for any logistic model. Non-linear models bend the boundary, but each one brings a new knob that can memorise noise, and a higher cost per prediction. This part shows the boundary each model draws, its knob, and the rule for climbing: move up a rung only when the test score says so. Part 7 then puts the winner inside on_tick.

When a line is not enough, a model has to bend the boundary: boxes (trees), a vote of boxes (forests), a chain of small corrections (boosting), a margin in a lifted space (SVM), or a learned stack of curves (a neural network). Every one of them has a knob that trades a too-simple fit for memorised noise. Turn it, watch the test score, and stop where it stops improving.

Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample with random features, so their errors differ. Averaging many of them cancels much of that noise while keeping the bends. class MyBot(Trader):
    def __init__(self, *a, **kw):
        super().__init__(*a, **kw)
        try:
            self.model = joblib.load("models/signal_v3.joblib")   # once, scaler + model together
        except Exception:
            self.model = None                                     # no crash: fall back

    def on_tick(self, tick):
        x = self.features.update(tick)                            # O(1) rolling state
        if self.model is None:
            side = momentum_rule(x)                               # the baseline still trades
        else:
            side = self.model.predict(x.reshape(1, -1))[0]        # microseconds, never fit() here
        self.trade(self.risk.size(side, tick))
Budget = the time until the next tick. Measure predict() with time.perf_counter_ns() on one row, not on a batch: a 500-tree forest is fast per batch and slow per call.

Your 500-tree forest takes 420 µs per call and ticks arrive every 300 µs. What happens, and what do you do?

Every tick waits behind the previous one, the queue grows and ticks are dropped or acted on late. Use a lighter model, or check that the forest really beats the light one after fees; do not call it per row in the loop.

The model file is missing on Friday morning. What should your bot do?

Catch the load error in __init__, set the model to None, and trade the baseline rule. A bot that crashes earns nothing; a bot on its fallback still trades.

Your live hit rate has been under 50% for 12 trades in a row. Retrain inside on_tick?

No. Retraining takes milliseconds to seconds and the loop has microseconds. Retrain offline on recent data, save a new versioned file, and reload it between ticks or between sessions.

Part 8 · Unstructured data

From a headline to a feature

Why this part exists. Parts 1–7 used numbers: prices, the book, trades. Much of what moves a price arrives as text first. This part turns a headline into one more column, with the same rules as before: stamp it with the time you received it, merge it as-of, test it with an event study, and keep it only if it makes the strategy better. After this part, the close and the Dec 4 block, where the whole pipeline runs on the flow of the house desk’s trades.

Text is not a feature until a pipeline makes it one. Each headline is stamped with the time you received it, its source, tickers and topic, and duplicates are dropped. Then it gets a sentiment score, is merged as-of onto prices, checked with an event study, and turned into a decaying number per symbol that your signal can read.

The prices react at publish time, before your feed delivers the headline. Stamped at received time, the event study shows only the part of the move still ahead of you (+66 bp); stamped at publish time, it also counts the jump that happened before you could read it (+135 bp). That extra half is a leak, the same mistake as the merge station in Part 3. The half-life is a parameter like any window in Part 2: choose it on validation data, not on the test set.

Remember

Merge news as-of on the time you received it, never the time it was published, and test it with an event study after fees. The usable feature is a decayed sentiment per symbol, updated in O(1).

In your bot: keep one number per symbol, s = s * 0.5 ** (dt / half_life) + score, and read it in on_tick.

What to notice

A general sentiment model does not know finance: “beats” means nothing to it and “cuts” sounds bad. Training only the head on 81 labeled finance headlines takes it from 47% to 80%; six labels on the missing phrasing take it to 100% on this small test set. Wrong labels teach the wrong thing just as fast. The page uses a bag-of-words head as a stand-in; the lab does it with distilbert in a few minutes on a laptop CPU.

Remember

Fine-tuning keeps a pretrained model and trains its last layer on labeled examples from your domain. A few hundred good finance labels beat a bigger general model; check the licence before you train on vendor news.

A headline is published at 10:00:00 and reaches your feed at 10:00:03. Which time goes into the merge, and why?

10:00:03, the time you received it. The move between 10:00:00 and 10:00:03 was traded by someone faster; stamping at 10:00:00 puts it in your backtest and inflates the event study (here +135 bp instead of +66).

Why can a word-count lexicon call “Meta loss narrows as ad revenue recovers” neutral or negative?

It counts words, not meaning: “loss” is negative and “narrows” is not in the list. Phrases, negation and “but” need a model that reads the sentence, or a finance-specific list.

What does fine-tuning change, and what does it need?

It keeps the pretrained body and trains the classification head (sometimes the last layers) on labeled examples from your domain. It needs correct labels on the phrasing you trade, and data you are allowed to train on.
