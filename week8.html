WEEK 7

Machine Learning Signals & Model Ops

A model is just another signal — a number your bot still has to size, risk-manage and route. This week you learn to earn that number honestly: features known at time t, splits that respect time, and a validated artifact that predicts inside your per-tick budget.

  • Build a feature matrix from prices and the order book without leaking the future into a single column.
  • Validate walk-forward and recognise the exact moment a backtest score stops being a measurement.
  • Operate a model: persist it, load it once, predict in on_tick, and watch it drift.
What is it?

Supervised learning, pointed at the future

Learn a mapping f(X) → y from historical examples. In trading, X is everything you know now; y is the piece of the future you are predicting.

Every row of your dataset is one point in time. The features in that row must be computable from data that had already printed at that instant — the last mid, the rolling volatility over the previous twenty ticks, the current book imbalance. The label belongs to the future: the return from t to t+h, or merely its sign.

Two label choices, two different jobs. Classification predicts direction and is robust — you can size by the predicted probability. Regression predicts magnitude, is more informative, and is noisier and harder. Either way the horizon h must match how long you actually intend to hold: a label about the next 30 seconds cannot justify a position you keep for an hour.

The golden rule has one clause. Every feature in X must be knowable at t. Anything else is leakage, and leakage does not look like a bug — it looks like a great model.

# one row per point-in-time observation
X = features known AT time t     # no future data!
y = label about t+h              # the horizon

# classification  -> sign of the next return
y = 1 if ret(t, t+h) > 0 else 0
# regression      -> magnitude of the next return
y = ret(t, t+h)

model.fit(X_train, y_train)
y_hat = model.predict(X_now)     # -> a signal

Why it is dangerous here

low SNR

Three properties of market data that make ML in finance different from ML on images.

Signal-to-noise
~52%
Returns are mostly noise. A model at 52% out-of-sample can be excellent — or pure overfit. You cannot tell by looking at it.
Non-stationarity
drift
The data-generating process changes. Yesterday's edge decays and a model trained on it rots silently, still returning confident numbers.
Feedback & scarcity
n small
Little independent data, and your own orders move the price. Backtests flatter; the live session collects.
Simple first. Every W3–W6 signal is a baseline. ML has to beat it out-of-sample, after costs to earn its place in your bot.

How does it work?

Splitting in time is the whole discipline

Fitting the model is one line. Everything that decides whether the number means anything happens in how you cut the data — and in how many times you look.

Random shuffling is the default in every tutorial and it is catastrophic on a time series. Shuffle, and a row from next Tuesday sits in your training set while its neighbour — nearly the same market state — sits in the test set. The model does not learn structure; it learns to recognise its neighbours. Your test score becomes a measurement of how similar adjacent ticks are, which is close to 100% and completely useless.

Why the embargo. A label at time t looks h ticks ahead, so rows
    within h of the boundary see across the wall even under a clean chronological split. Purging those rows
    costs you a handful of samples and buys you a number you can trust.

  


  
Once the wall is in the right place, one split is still not an evaluation — it is a single sample. Walk-forward
  (rolling-origin) validation trains on the past, tests on the next unseen block, rolls forward and repeats. Every test
  block is out-of-sample, which is exactly the situation your bot is in at every tick of the live session: it only ever
  knows the past.

    Why does it matter in trading?
    
Accuracy is a vanity metric. Net, risk-adjusted P&L is the score.

    
A 55% classifier that flips every tick pays the spread endlessly and finishes the season below idle
    cash. A 52% classifier that trades forty times pays for itself.

  


  
The arena settles this argument with numbers. Every fill pays a taker fee or earns a maker rebate, every crossing of
  the spread costs half of it, and the season leaderboard ranks risk-adjusted return — not raw P&L. So the
  question a model has to answer is never "is it accurate?" but "does its edge per trade survive the cost of the trades
  it wants to make?"

Turnover is a design parameter, not an accident. Raise the confidence threshold in
    on_tick and you trade less, pay less, and keep more of a smaller edge. Slower, surer signals win seasons
    in this arena because the score divides by volatility.

  


  

    

      
Train offline · load once · predict in on_tick

      
The split that keeps you inside the latency budget: heavy work outside the loop, only
      predict() inside it.

      
# team/trader.py
import joblib
from arena import Trader, Signal

class MLTrader(Trader):
    def __init__(self):
        self.model = joblib.load('model.pkl')   # load ONCE

    def on_tick(self, market, portfolio):
        for sym in market.symbols():
            px = market.prices(sym)
            if len(px) < 21: continue
            X = features(px, market, sym)        # same fn as offline
            p = self.model.predict_proba([X])[0][1]
            if p > 0.60 and portfolio.can_buy(sym, 5, market.mid_price(sym)):
                return Signal(symbol=sym, side='buy', quantity=5,
                              price=market.best_ask(sym), confidence=p)
        return None

    Common mistakes
    
Five ways to produce a beautiful, worthless model

    
Every one of these has shipped in a real fund. Each expands.

  


  

    train_test_split(shuffle=True) on a time series
    
The tutorial default. Adjacent ticks are nearly identical, so a shuffled split puts a row's
      near-twin on the other side of the wall. The model memorises neighbours and reports accuracy in the 70s and 80s.
      The tell: a test score far above anything the literature reports for return prediction.
      
Fix: cut by index, never by permutation — X[:cut] / X[cut:] —
      and purge the h rows that straddle the boundary.

  


  

    A feature that quietly contains its own label
    
A rolling calculation centred instead of trailing, a column derived from the close of the bar you
      are predicting, a "current session VWAP" that includes the future. Silent and fatal: the model looks brilliant
      offline and random live. The tell: one feature dominates the importance ranking and the edge collapses the
      instant you go live.
      
Fix: for each feature ask "at exactly which timestamp could I have computed this?" Then compute
      features with the same function offline and inside on_tick — one function, two callers.

  


  

    Tuning on the test block (data snooping)
    
Try a hundred configurations, keep the best test score, report it. You have now fitted the test set;
      its score is a maximum over noise, not a measurement. The published number is inflated by roughly the spread of
      your hundred attempts. The tell: your best configuration is nowhere near your second best.
      
Fix: tune on a validation block, look at the test block once, log every experiment you
      ran, and deflate your Sharpe for the number of trials.

  


  

    Feature mismatch between training and on_tick
    
Offline you standardised with the full-sample mean and standard deviation; live you standardise with
      a rolling one. Offline the imbalance used total depth; live it uses top-of-book. The model receives inputs it has
      never seen and outputs confident nonsense. The tell: live predictions cluster at one extreme.
      
Fix: persist the scaler and the feature list with the model, and assert the live feature
      vector's length and order on the first tick.

  


  

    A model you cannot run inside a tick
    
A gradient-boosted forest of 2,000 trees, re-fitted every tick "to stay fresh", loading the pickle
      inside on_tick. Your bot misses the market it correctly predicted, and W8's rate limits throttle the
      backlog. The tell: your fills print at prices two ticks behind your signals.
      
Fix: load once in __init__, time predict() against the per-tick budget
      from W5, and prefer a small model you can actually run. A model you can't run live scores nothing.

Three questions

    
Instant feedback, no grading. Pick the answer you would defend in a demo.

  


  

    
Question 1

    
Your classifier reports 84% out-of-sample accuracy on next-tick direction for AAPL. What is the
      single most likely explanation?

    

      
      
      
      
    

    
C. Next-tick direction on a liquid name is close to a coin flip; the honest range is roughly
      50–55%. An 84% is not a good model, it is a diagnostic: something on the other side of the wall got into
      training. Check the split first, then hunt the feature.

  


  

    
Question 2

    
You label with a horizon of h = 10 ticks and split chronologically at 70%. Why is an
      embargo still needed?

    

      
      
      
      
    

    
B. The wall stops features, not labels. A label is about the future, so rows within
      h of the cut straddle it. Purge those h rows and the boundary is genuinely sealed —
      the cost is a handful of samples.

  


  

    
Question 3

    
Two bots, same walk-forward hit rate of 53%. Bot A trades 30 times a session, bot B trades 350 times.
      Round-trip cost is 3 bp. Who ranks higher on the season leaderboard, and why?

    

      
      
      
      
    

    
D. Expectancy is per trade and small; costs are per trade and certain. Multiply both by 350 and
      the certain term wins. On top of that, exchange/scoring.py ranks return per unit of volatility with a
      drawdown penalty, so the churn is punished twice.
